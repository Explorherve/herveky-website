Rely on reality and don’t lie, you’ll come up with realistic financial projections

Dear reader,

I recently asked a founder after she described to me her projections: “ How did you come up with this number of items you will sell?”

“Honestly, I just came up with the number”, she told me.

She went on to tell me that she wasn’t confident and asked me how to do realistic projections when you haven’t made any sales yet.

In this letter, I will share with the 5 ways I shared with her to make realistic projections and that you can use straight away.

I will also show you what to avoid, so you are confident with your numbers and don’t come up with unrealistic financial projections.

Let’s dive into the 5 ways to prepare realistic projections.

Use industry average to start with (#1)

When I was looking at companies' financial projections in my previous career as an investment banker, I used to compare them with industry averages.

So if you don’t have historical numbers, you can use the industry averages for your assumptions to come up with realistic projections.

For example, with one founder I worked with, we wanted to plan how many people would convert from visiting her website to buying her underwear.

We found out that, in her industry, the average percentage of visitors converting into buyers is between 3 and 5% for new brands.

So, we used a conservative 3% conversion rate in the first months.

But you don’t just want to be average, otherwise what makes you unique?

Therefore you can look to beat the industry average

Beating the industry average means that you are doing better than the average player in your industry.

But to make this realistic, you need to be able to explain the reason.

For example, going back to the underwear example, the founder has a community of women who support each other after going through surgery, so whenever these people land on her website, they are high-intent clients. They are not coming cold from an ad, they know the brand.

That makes the conversion rate realistically higher, closer to 10% rather than the 3-5% of the industry average.

On some occasions, the industry average data might not be enough to be realistic. That’s when you can use the results of your own experiments.

Using your results from experiments (#2)

For example, my partner’s mentee is a founder of a dating app. He launched an ad on Instagram for 1 week to see how many people would click on it.

This then informs him, in a realistic way, what the cost per click is.

You can do that even if you don’t have a product launched yet.

All you need to do is to design an ad that explains what your product does and then invite the viewers to learn more by going to your website.

To get the cost per click, you divide the amount of money you spent on the ads, for example £100, by the number of visits, which, let’s say, is 50. Then, in that case, the cost is £2 per click.

But sometimes you don’t even need to spend money on experiments.

You can use your own insights from conversations with your clients (#3)

The founder selling underwear learned from her first customers that they need, at least, two pairs of underwear. One for the day and one for the night.

So to estimate the average number of items bought by her customers, this is a realistic data she can use.

You might not have insights for all parts of the business, so you can use your own marketing budget constraints.

Use your marketing budget constraints (#4)

When you know your marketing budget and your customer acquisition cost, you can derive how much you can spend to get more customers and ultimately how much you can grow.

For example, if a business owner selling fashion clothes has a budget of £25k for marketing for a year, with a customer acquisition cost of £12, and an average spend per customer of £100, the realistic sales she can make are about £200k a year.

What if you grow organically?

But you might say you only have sales coming from organic channels.

For example, a friend of mine sells cosmetic products. She posts 8 times a day on Facebook and TikTok and doesn’t do any ads.

All of her customers come from these posts.

So if that’s the case for you as well, estimate the hours you spend doing the posts at your hourly rate, and how many people come after 1 month of posting.

By dividing those two data, you have your customer acquisition cost and can realistically derive how much growth you can generate from the organic channels.

So your growth can come from the marketing but growth might also come from improvements in your products.

Let’s see how realistic this can be.

Tie your growth projections to your products launch (#5)

If you plan to make a better product, a better workflow for your customers, and you have some proof that this can realistically be driving a higher conversion rate, then use that method.

For instance, in my previous startup, when we launched a new feature, we always had an uptick in new users signing up because people were curious to see what was new.

So we took this into account in our projections.

Now that I have shared with you 5 ways to prepare realistic financial projections, I want to share with you what unrealistic projections are so that you avoid them.

What are unrealistic projections?

Unrealistic projections are numbers you can’t explain. I’ve put below 3 examples that I see lead founders to come up with numbers they can’t fully explain.

Using market penetration without knowing how to get there.

I had a founder who was showing a 1% market penetration, but she was not showing how she would get there.

What I like to do is to put myself in the shoes of an investor and ask myself, “How the hell will they penetrate the market”?

Market penetration is a good approximation of the potential of the market.

But using it solely doesn’t make your projections realistic if you don’t show how you are going to get there.

Numbers without having a logic behind

As I wrote at the beginning of this letter, if your answer, when asked about the source of your numbers, is “honestly, I just came up with this number” then you probably don’t have any logic behind your numbers.

If you don’t have a logic behind the numbers, actually you don’t know whether they are realistic or not. You’re probably guessing.

But people, especially investors, want certainty, because certainty removes doubts. When you remove the doubts, it’s easier for you to commit to doing something.

So if you remove most of the doubts for an investor, you increase your chances of them giving you money.

And to keep increasing your chances of getting money from investors and from customers, avoid lying to yourself.

Don’t use numbers you don’t believe in

When I prepared my own financial projections for my first business, I caught myself trying to show exponential growth, that means revenue going from £10,000 to £5 millions in 3 years.

Not because I believed in it but because I thought investors wanted to see that.

But I realised what would happen in a meeting with an investor:

“How do you expect to grow by 500x in 3 years ?” the investor would ask.

“Well, you tell me” I’d scream inside my brain, before coming up with a vague explanation that I didn’t even believe in myself, something like:

“Well, we expect the word-of-mouth to really take off and every person will bring 3 or 4 friends, so the referral loop leads to exponential growth”.

As much as it might become true, if you don’t believe in your numbers, they will be unrealistic by definition.

To make realistic projections, base yourself on reality and avoid lying to yourself.

As I covered in this letter, there are 5 ways to do so: using industry average, your own experiments, your insights based on real conversations with customers, your marketing budget constraints and tying your assumptions to your product development.

On the other hand, avoid using numbers that you can’t explain, that have no logic or that you don’t believe in.

If you can’t explain where the number is coming from, then it’s a signal that the number is not based on reality.

As French philosopher Descartes said, “I think, therefore I am”. You can apply the same mindset when preparing your financial projections.

The more realistically you can come up with your financial projections, the closer you’ll be to making them happen.

The more you’ll make them happen, the quicker you will reach profitability and be investment-ready!
