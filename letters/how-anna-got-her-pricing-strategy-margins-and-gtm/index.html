How I got Anna her pricing strategy, margins and GTM by looking just at her numbers

Dear reader,

A founder selling physical items, Anna, reached out to me as she wanted to understand how to use her business numbers to make informed decisions and run a profitable business.

In this letter, I will show you the before and after Anna’s understanding of her numbers and how this allowed her to establish a pricing strategy, understand the right margins to make and know what go-to-market channels to prioritise.

I will also show you that you can’t run a financially sustainable business if you don’t understand your numbers early.

So, let’s start with the story of Anna.

Anna was pre-launch when she first contacted me.

Before Anna started the process of understanding her numbers, she didn’t know how to use her numbers to make decisions that would help her run a profitable business.

When talking about her product to her clients, the same question kept coming up: “What is your pricing?”. Investors asked her, “Where do you sit in the market?”.

She said she was premium but didn’t really know what premium meant in terms of pricing. She had provisional prices but wasn’t sure about them.

Despite having calculated her landed and production costs, she still couldn’t come up with her retail pricing and her wholesale pricing. And she didn’t know what margins she should be working towards.

All of this led her to urgently stop guessing her pricing and come up with a structure.

We looked into her manufacturing costs, customer acquisition costs, selling costs and her business costs

And we compared these costs to the provisional prices she had in mind.

By doing this, we established the minimum price at which she could sell her product without losing money.

But this was not enough.

We calculated how many units she needed to sell to be profitable depending on her pricing.

What did this tell us? Two things.

On the one hand, selling at a higher price means fewer units need to be sold to be profitable, but she might be less competitive compared to the market.

On the other hand, selling at a lower price means that she needs to sell more units to be profitable. But she might be more competitive in terms of pricing for her customers.

Now she could see more clearly the consequences of each pricing for her business.

However, she still had doubts about the margins she needed to make.

“Are my margins enough?”, she asked me. “It depends on your objectives”, I replied.

Let me explain my reasoning.

Understand the margins you need to make

For example, if her objective is to increase the availability of her product through partner boutiques, she doesn’t necessarily need to optimise her margins.

She can sell at a lower price to be competitive, build a brand and then sell at a higher price once she has a stronger brand.

Let me show you with numbers.

Let’s look at how the margins for the boutiques work. For instance, if a boutique sells her product for £100 and buys it for £50, then the boutique makes £50 on each unit.

Now imagine that her brand awareness and attractiveness have increased.

She can increase the pricing from £50 to £70, for example, because now she has leverage. The boutique profits will be reduced to £30.

But how can the boutique accept that?

Because now she has the argument that she can drive traffic to the boutique. This means they need to spend less on marketing. Or, to put it another way, she has the power to include an implied marketing cost in her pricing.

So, by understanding her numbers, she can make an informed decision on the margins she needs to make, depending on her strategy and the leverage she has with her clients.

Prioritise your go-to-market channels by understanding your numbers

Another example of how to use your numbers to make informed business decisions is to determine which go-to-market channel to prioritise.

For example, with Anna, we compared the customer acquisition cost per unit when selling directly through her website with the cost of selling to boutiques.

We found out that the marketing cost of selling on the website was higher than that of selling to the boutiques.

However, the margins from selling on the website were higher than those from selling to the boutiques.

So, which one should she prioritise?

Although the margins were higher when selling directly through her website, the initial marketing investment was 3 times higher than the one for selling to the boutiques.

So we concluded that, at her stage, it’d be better for her to prioritise the boutiques.

This is how understanding your numbers can lead you to make informed business decisions and run a profitable business.

Now, in a world where you don’t use numbers to make decisions, what would happen?

When I started my first business, I overlooked the numbers. I didn’t use them as a decision-making tool. Yes, I had financial projections, but I mainly used them for our pitch deck, not to make decisions for our business.

Instead, I was guessing which channels to target and what price would be good enough.

This cost us 6 months of runway.

It’s only when I met one of my mentors, a former founder, that I saw the power of numbers to make informed decisions. Then I was able to run the business in an informed way and raise money on the back of it.

The earlier you understand your numbers, the sooner you can build a profitable business

Unlike me, you’d be better off understanding your numbers earlier rather than later because this is an asset.

And, like any asset, it compounds.

When you understand your numbers, you can turn each of your business decisions into a more informed one over time.

But if you don’t understand your numbers, it becomes a debt, and it compounds in the wrong direction.

The more time you spend not understanding your numbers, the more uninformed decisions you will make. The business will suffer, and so will you, and I don’t want you to suffer!

Understanding your numbers is your competitive advantage

Understanding the numbers allowed Anna to make informed business decisions. She went from guessing her numbers to having a clear framework for her pricing strategy. She now understands the margins she needs to make and knows which channels to target.

Ultimately, she feels more confident about launching her product, and speaking about her numbers in public and with investors.

Understanding the numbers early is a real advantage for you as a founder. The earlier you know how your business works to make a profit, the sooner you will be able to make it.

And once you make a profit, you attract more customers because you have the budget to do so. The more customers you attract, the more investment you can attract, and so on.
